About five years ago, I began working on Bitcoin’s open-source software. I stopped contributing about a year ago when I began college. A year of meeting new people has given me the opportunity to think about how different working in open-source is from most jobs. A lot of people have asked me about what it was like, and I sometimes have difficulty articulating the unique challenges that came with it, but also the love that I have for open-source and the Bitcoin development community. I figured it would be useful to document this somewhere. 
<br><br>
It is worth elaborating on what “Bitcoin Core Development” is. Bitcoin Core is the open-source software behind Bitcoin. It is what most people are running when they say that they are “running a Bitcoin node”. The development of this software happens on GitHub, and the people who open and review pull requests are called “Bitcoin Core Developers”. Because it is very important software that requires precision and attention to detail, it is typical for a PR to require review from at least three developers (or a lot more if it is a large protocol change). When I refer to “making contributions”, I mean reviewing code and opening PRs. 
<br><br>
How I came to be involved in this field is kind of a long story, but to abbreviate: I read a book about Bitcoin from the library, read the whitepaper, went to a developer meetup, and long story short, began making contributions my freshman year of high school. In addition to these contributions, I also did Bitcoin privacy research that I published on my website. I’m glossing over this because it is not as interesting as what follows. 
<br><br>
Contributing to open-source while still technically being a full-time student was incredibly fun. I used to skip class to review PRs in the hallway. I remember once sitting in the back of my algebra class with my laptop, participating in an IRC meeting being held to review one of my PRs, which was happening at the same time. I also used to take calls from in front of my locker, while trying to hide my AirPods from the deans who were notorious for confiscating them (to be fair, we were not allowed to have them in school; of course, this stopped absolutely nobody). Bitcoin Core has many very specific code review conventions that take some getting used to, but I found getting accustomed to this new system exciting. 
<br><br>
	I also never particularly watched the price of Bitcoin. My focus was always on the technology, on the protocols being written and the code implementing them. This distinction between the community of engineers actually working on the software and the people on so-called “Bitcoin Twitter” was also difficult to explain to others. I was once wearing a hoodie I got at a Bitcoin conference when my math teacher started telling me about how I was losing him money. I had been working on this stuff for about two years and was tired of having these conversations, so I lazily just laughed and said I was sorry. 
<br><br>
I spent a lot of time grappling with how to frame open-source development for the people reading my college applications. College admissions tend to emphasize various forms of competition, be it science olympiad, sports, etc. This is very reasonable. Getting into college is itself a competition where you are competing with thousands of students from around the country (and the world). After three years of working in open-source, competition felt like a foreign concept to me. The type of open-source development I was doing was an extremely collaborative environment, in contrast to a lot of the competitive things that my peers were doing (no disrespect to competitions, just noting the difference).
<br><br>
	Open-source does not care about who you are. The emphasis is always on how you are helping improve the technology. It is not about prestige (many developers are pseudoanonymous) or money (a lot of developers don’t get paid). While people thought it was cool that I was on the younger side, my work spoke for itself, and as a result, in technical spaces, I was taken seriously. This is what gave me the confidence to propose changes to code written by very experienced developers. This is also why I felt qualified to publish research critiquing how a variety of the most popular wallets construct transactions.
<br><br>
A founder whom I really respect recently asked me why I didn’t start a company based on my privacy research. The thought hadn’t even crossed my mind. I had spent so long surrounded by people working in public and publishing their findings for everyone. I literally grew up learning that one should approach an industry with the mentality of “what specifically can I contribute to improve this technology and the lives of the people who use it”. 
<br><br>
	I just finished my freshman year at MIT studying electrical engineering. Given my previous interest in systems-level programming, I’ve been getting more interested in hardware and plan on doing hardware research this summer. I’m incredibly grateful to all of the developers and researchers I met while working on Bitcoin, who helped me have these experiences. I still miss open-source development. I miss seeing someone I really look up to review my PR, I miss scouring others’ code to find bugs, and I miss seeing my name in the release notes. And yet, because I spent those years contributing to open-source during a formative period of my life, I’m certain that whatever I choose to work on in the future will always be shaped by the time I spent as a Bitcoin Core developer.
